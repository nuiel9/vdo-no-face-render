In 1957, an 18-year-old named Yvon Chouinard set up a coal forge in his parents' backyard in Burbank, California, and started making climbing pitons by hand. Each one took about an hour to forge. He sold them at Yosemite Valley for $1.50 each, out of the back of his car, to anyone who needed a reliable piece of steel hammered into a crack in the granite. He was not thinking about building a company. He was thinking about getting up the wall.

By the late 1960s, Chouinard Equipment had grown from that backyard forge into the largest supplier of climbing hardware in the United States. The catalog was thin. The prices were fair. Every serious American rock climber knew the name, and most of them were hammering Chouinard steel into the same routes every season.

Then Chouinard and his climbing partner Tom Frost looked at what was actually happening to the rock.

The most popular routes on El Capitan and Half Dome were deteriorating. Steel pitons, hammered in and yanked out by hundreds of climbers across hundreds of days, were permanently widening the granite cracks. Routes that had been clean in the late 1950s were visibly scarred by 1970. The damage was real, it was accelerating, and the largest supplier of the tools causing it was Chouinard Equipment.

Chouinard and Frost wrote a response. They published it in the Chouinard Equipment catalog in the early 1970s. The essay, titled "Clean Climbing," ran approximately 14 pages, and its argument was straightforward: climbers should abandon steel pitons entirely in favor of aluminum chocks that could be placed by hand and removed without touching the rock. No hammering. No permanent damage. No trace left behind.

This was not a PR exercise. Chouinard Equipment was the largest piton supplier in the country, and the two men who wrote the essay were the same men who manufactured and sold the product they were asking climbers to abandon. According to Chouinard's memoir "Let My People Go Surfing," published in 2005, they expected the essay to be financially painful.

Within a year, the US climbing community had largely converted to chocks. Chouinard Equipment developed and sold the new aluminum protection. Revenue held. The company had bent the market toward its values rather than bending its values toward the market. That sequence would repeat itself for the next five decades.

The clothing side of the business began by accident. Chouinard had been wearing a rugby shirt on climbing trips, the kind manufactured by a British sportswear company for field sports, heavy cotton, reinforced collar, because it was the toughest shirt available. Other climbers kept asking where he got it. In the early 1970s, Chouinard Equipment ordered a run of rugby shirts and listed them in the catalog. They sold quickly. The number was modest, around 200 units, but it was enough to register as something worth investigating.

In 1973, Chouinard incorporated the clothing side as a separate company. He named it after the region at the southern tip of South America. In his memoir, he described the name as one that is "resonant of romance, wild windswept landscapes and adventure." It had no trademark conflicts. It fit the image he had in mind: the gear you would need at the end of the world, made by people who had actually been there.

What Chouinard did not know in 1973 was that the framework he had established in the piton years, sacrificing short-term revenue to hold a value, would eventually produce one of the most unusual advertisements in American corporate history.

Patagonia's early years in Ventura, California, were defined by a tension that most companies resolve quickly. How do you sell outdoor gear to people you are simultaneously telling to consume less? Chouinard never resolved it. He built a company that lived inside that tension instead.

In 1986, Patagonia committed to donating one percent of total sales, not profits, to environmental organizations. The distinction is structural. A company that donates from profits can hide the commitment in a bad year. A company that donates from revenue is committed regardless. Patagonia has maintained that commitment for nearly four decades.

In 2002, Chouinard co-founded 1% for the Planet with Craig Mathews, extending the model to other businesses. By the time of the 2022 ownership transfer, more than 6,000 companies had joined the network.

But the moment that drew the sharpest outside attention came on Black Friday, November 25, 2011. Patagonia published a full-page advertisement in the New York Times. The headline read: "Don't Buy This Jacket." Below it was an image of one of their best-selling fleece products, followed by a precise accounting of its environmental cost: 135 liters of water consumed in production, enough greenhouse gas emissions to drive 45 miles, 60 percent of its weight generated as waste during manufacturing. The ad closed with a request that customers not buy more than they need.

This was the highest-revenue retail day in the United States. Patagonia chose it to run a full-page advertisement in the country's most-read newspaper asking their customers to spend less money.

The following year, Patagonia's revenue grew.

The mechanism is not complicated, though it looks paradoxical. Patagonia's core customers are not casual buyers. They pay a significant premium precisely because the company appears to share their values. When Patagonia publicly sacrificed a potential sale in the name of its environmental commitments, those customers became more loyal, not less. The "Don't Buy This Jacket" campaign is now studied in business programs as a case in counterintuitive brand strategy. Chouinard would likely argue the point: it was not strategy. It was what they believed. The fact that belief and strategy produced the same outcome is the part worth examining.

In September 2022, Chouinard announced the ownership transfer. The press release headline: "Earth Is Now Our Only Shareholder." The Holdfast Collective, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit, now receives all of Patagonia's annual profits, directed toward climate work. The Patagonia Purpose Trust, controlled by the Chouinard family, retains all voting shares. Governance stays with the family. The economic value goes to the cause. The structure was designed to be difficult to unwind, using a purpose trust rather than a foundation to make the commitment effectively permanent.

What started with $1.50 pitons in a Burbank backyard had become a company valued at approximately $3 billion, generating an estimated $100 million annually for environmental causes, built on a business model that repeatedly gave up short-term revenue to protect the one asset that made the whole thing work: credibility with people who care more about what a company does than what it charges.

The lesson is not that anti-consumerism sells. It is that authenticity, practiced at the moment it costs something, compounds over decades into the kind of trust that no advertising budget can manufacture.
