# REVIEW: The Leather Jacket Deal That Launched Patagonia
Daily #28 | 2026-05-09 | Story template | business-pm-28

## Thesis

Patagonia did not succeed despite its anti-consumerism stance. It succeeded because of it. The founding arc, from hand-forged steel pitons in a Burbank backyard to a 14-page catalog essay urging climbers to stop buying the company's own product, established a pattern in which sacrificing short-term revenue to hold a value generated long-term brand loyalty that no advertising budget could replicate.

## Contrarian Angles

1. The "Clean Climbing" catalog essay was the actual founding moment, not the clothing launch. Patagonia's identity crystallized when Chouinard Equipment publicly undermined its own piton business. The rugby shirt was a downstream consequence. Template: Story. **SELECTED.**

2. "Don't Buy This Jacket" was brand engineering disguised as ethics. The campaign deepened loyalty in a premium-price segment, and revenue grew the following year. Treating it as a sincere moral stand misses the structural mechanism. Template: Hidden Truth.

3. The 2022 donation preserved family control. The Chouinard family retained 100% of voting shares through the Patagonia Purpose Trust while surrendering economic value to the Holdfast Collective. "Gave away the company" overstates the transfer. Template: Hidden Truth.

4. Patagonia's supply chain undercuts its environmental messaging. The company's own Environmental + Social Initiatives reports document manufacturing emissions from Asian factories. Template: Hidden Truth.

5. The piton-to-clothing pivot had a survival dimension alongside the values dimension. Product liability exposure from climbing accidents was a real risk to Chouinard Equipment by the early 1970s. Template: Story.

**Rationale for Selected:** Angle 1 ties directly to the "deal" framing in the title, is fully sourced from Chouinard's memoir, and subverts typical Patagonia brand-worship coverage by centering the hardware business, not the jacket.

## Six Lesser-Known Facts

1. The "Clean Climbing" essay in the Chouinard Equipment catalog was approximately 14 pages long, co-written by Chouinard and climbing partner Tom Frost. They were the largest piton supplier in the US at the time of publication. CITATION: "Let My People Go Surfing," 2005. FLAG: Verify exact page count and catalog year (sources vary between 1972 and 1973).

2. Chouinard sold his first pitons for $1.50 each out of the back of his car at Yosemite. By the late 1960s, Chouinard Equipment was the largest climbing hardware supplier in the United States. CITATION: "Let My People Go Surfing," 2005. FLAG: Verify the $1.50 figure against the memoir.

3. Patagonia has donated 1% of total sales (not profits) to environmental organizations since 1986, sixteen years before Chouinard co-founded the 1% for the Planet network with Craig Mathews in 2002. CITATION: 1percentfortheplanet.org; Patagonia company history.

4. The "Don't Buy This Jacket" full-page ad ran in the New York Times on Black Friday, November 25, 2011. Patagonia's revenue reportedly grew from approximately $400 million that year to $543 million the following year. CITATION: Patagonia press release; trade press. FLAG: Revenue figures are from trade reporting (company is private); verify best available sourcing.

5. The 2022 ownership transfer gave all profits and dividends to the Holdfast Collective (501(c)(4)) while the Patagonia Purpose Trust, controlled by the Chouinard family, retained all voting shares. Governance control remained with the family. CITATION: Patagonia press release, September 14, 2022.

6. The name "Patagonia" was chosen in 1973 partly for trademark availability. Chouinard needed a name that evoked wild landscapes and had no existing trademark conflicts. "Patagonia" cleared both criteria. CITATION: "Let My People Go Surfing," 2005.
