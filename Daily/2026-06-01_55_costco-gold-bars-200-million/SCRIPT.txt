In April 2024, a finance YouTuber named Humphrey Yang walked into Witter Coin, a bullion dealer in San Francisco, carrying a one-ounce PAMP Suisse gold bar in its original assay card. He had bought the bar a year earlier at Costco for two thousand three hundred fifty-nine dollars. Witter Coin paid him two thousand nine hundred fifty-five dollars and forty-two cents in cash. Yang walked out with a five hundred ninety-five dollar profit. He filmed the entire transaction on his phone, posted it to TikTok, and the video has been watched six and a half million times.

What Yang's video confirmed for an audience of curious YouTubers and Reddit users was something Wall Street had been quietly tracking for nine months. Costco Wholesale Corporation, the membership-based warehouse retailer best known for jumbo packages of paper towels and a one-dollar-fifty hot dog, had quietly turned itself into one of the largest retail gold dealers in the United States. There was no press release. No marketing campaign. Just a quiet listing on costco dot com starting in June 2023.

In December 2023, on Costco's first-quarter fiscal year 2024 earnings call, the company's then-chief-financial-officer Richard Galanti casually mentioned that the company had sold roughly one hundred million dollars in one-ounce gold bars over the previous three months. He said it in passing, in the middle of a list of product categories that had performed well. The earnings call attendees noticed. The trade press noticed. The Reddit warehouse-shopping subforum noticed faster than any of them.

By April 2024, Wells Fargo equity analyst Edward Kelly published a research note estimating that Costco's monthly gold-bar revenue had grown to between one hundred million and two hundred million dollars. His exact words, from the note: "There has been significant interest given Costco's aggressive pricing and high level of customer trust." Kelly cited the accelerating frequency of Reddit posts, the rapid online sell-outs of new product drops, and Costco's robust monthly e-commerce sales as evidence of, in his words, "a sharp uptick in momentum since the launch."

The mechanic was simple. Costco partnered with two of the largest bullion refiners in the world. PAMP Suisse, a Swiss refiner based in Castel San Pietro and accredited by the London Bullion Market Association, the global standards body that maintains the Good Delivery List defining what investment-grade bullion actually means. And Rand Refinery, the South African refiner founded in 1920 that has historically processed roughly a third of all the gold ever mined, more than any other single facility in the world. Costco listed both refiners' one-ounce bars on its website. Each bar weighed thirty-one and a fraction grams of point-nine-nine-nine-nine fine gold, sealed in a tamper-evident assay card with a unique serial number. Each bar was priced at approximately two percent above the live spot price of gold on the day Costco's listing was updated.

That two-percent margin was the entire story.

For a customer with a Costco Executive Membership and a stacked credit card setup, the two-percent retail markup could be offset by two percent in membership cash-back, two or three percent in credit card cash-back, and in some configurations another two percent through services that allowed credit cards to settle debit card payments. The math, run carefully, meant some customers could buy Costco gold at or below the live spot price. That is not a thing that exists anywhere else in retail bullion. Every other gold dealer in the United States typically charges between four percent and ten percent over spot for one-ounce bars. Costco was effectively offering customers wholesale bullion at retail-membership prices, with the warehouse club absorbing the entire dealer margin.

The next question is the obvious one. Why.

Wells Fargo's Kelly addressed it in the same note. The two-percent margin, after subtracting shipping cost and warehousing overhead, made gold bars, in his words, "a very low-profit business at best." Costco was not making meaningful money on each bar sold. At a hundred-million-dollar-per-month run rate, with gross margins that thin, the company was probably netting only a few million dollars per month on its gold business. In a quarter when Costco reported net income above one billion dollars, the gold-bar contribution was rounding error.

So Costco was running a category that produced rounding-error profit and selling out within hours of every product drop. That decision was not really about gold. It was about the same thing the one-dollar-fifty hot dog has been about for forty years.

Costco's average product markup is roughly fourteen percent, the lowest of any major US retailer. Walmart averages closer to twenty-five percent. Traditional grocery chains run thirty percent or higher. The reason Costco can hold its product margins that thin is that the company does not really make its operating profit from products. It makes most of its operating profit from the annual membership fees that customers pay for the privilege of walking through the door. In fiscal 2024, those membership fees totaled approximately four point eight billion dollars, and they account for the majority of the company's reported operating income.

That structural difference is what lets Costco offer gold at two percent over spot without the math breaking. The company is not trying to win on gold-bar margin. It is trying to renew memberships. The Costco brand promise is that the customer is always being given a deal that other retailers will not match. Selling gold at two percent over spot in a category where the average dealer charges six percent reinforces that promise. Every member who buys a bar tells a friend. Every member who flips a bar at a local dealer tells a story. Every member who never buys a bar but reads about the program on Reddit absorbs the underlying message. The renewal rate on Costco memberships in the United States has stayed at roughly ninety percent for two consecutive decades, partly because of brand promises like this one.

We covered McDonald's broken ice cream machines a few weeks ago. McDonald's lost a quiet competitive war against its own franchisees because it never controlled the manufacturer of the ice cream machine that defined its product experience. Costco is the opposite case study, in the same demographic, on the same brand mechanic. McDonald's gave up control of the mechanical layer of its consumer promise, and the system used that control against the brand. Costco took on the mechanical layer of an entirely new product category, accepted the thin margin, and used the control to extend the brand. Same demographic. Same brand-trust mechanic. Opposite outcome.

By April 2024, Costco had quietly added a purchase limit on gold bars, capping individual customers at five bars per order. Reddit moderators on the warehouse-shopping subforum pinned posts explaining the cash-back stacking math. Witter Coin and dozens of regional bullion dealers reported customers walking in with PAMP Suisse Costco bars and walking out with checks. Humphrey Yang's TikTok was just the one that caught the algorithm.

Richard Galanti retired as Costco's chief financial officer on March fifteenth, 2024, after thirty-nine years at the company. Gary Millerchip took over. The gold-bar program remained one of Costco's top online product categories through the rest of the year, alongside silver coins and platinum bars that the company added in late 2024.
