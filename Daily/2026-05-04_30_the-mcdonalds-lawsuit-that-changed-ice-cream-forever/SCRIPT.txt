In October 2020, a software engineer named Rashiq Zahid built a website called McBroken.com in a single afternoon. The site did one thing: it pinged McDonald's own mobile ordering app every thirty seconds and tested whether each U.S. location could accept a McFlurry order. On a typical day, the map showed roughly one in six American McDonald's locations returning a broken result. Zahid did not access any private system. He queried McDonald's public-facing API, the same endpoint the company's own app used when a customer tried to order dessert. McDonald's had built the very infrastructure that documented its own failure at scale.

McBroken went viral in under a week. The dataset appeared in the Washington Post, Business Insider, and food service trade publications. It converted a decade of collective frustration into a mapped, timestamped public record. Customers had long suspected the broken soft-serve machine was systemic. McBroken confirmed it was a national pattern with a measurable rate.

But the machine was not the problem. It was the readable symptom of a structural problem that had been compounding inside McDonald's franchise architecture for years before anyone had the data to say so clearly.

McDonald's operates more than 40,000 locations globally, with roughly 95 percent of U.S. restaurants owned by independent franchisees. When a franchisee signs a McDonald's franchise agreement, they do not choose their own suppliers. McDonald's requires purchasing from a list of approved vendors, disclosed in the Franchise Disclosure Document under Item 8. For soft-serve equipment, that approved list directed franchisees to Taylor Commercial Foodservice. In practice, across most of the U.S. system, Taylor was the only option on the list.

Taylor's C602 soft-serve machine became the system-wide standard. Every day, food safety regulations require that soft-serve machines run a heat treatment cycle, a pasteurization process that holds the mix at temperature long enough to eliminate pathogens. That requirement is real and codified in FDA food safety rules. What Taylor's implementation added was a layer the FDA does not require: if the cycle encountered any fault, a proprietary reset sequence locked the machine until an authorized Taylor service technician cleared it. A service visit ran hundreds of dollars. Scheduling one could take days. During that window the machine sat broken, and the franchise owner paid.

Franchisees who wanted to switch had signed a contract that made switching a breach. The approved-vendor clause in the McDonald's franchise agreement did not allow franchisees to evaluate competitors on performance or price. It required Taylor. Taylor's service pricing reflected the reality of operating in a market where its customers had no contractual exit.

Jeremy O'Sullivan and Melissa Nelson founded a company called Kytch to address exactly that gap. Their device attached to the Taylor C602's internal serial data port, a hardware interface that carried the machine's real-time diagnostic codes. The same codes that Taylor billed thousands of dollars in service contracts to interpret, Kytch translated immediately on a connected phone. Franchise owners who installed Kytch reported their machines remained operational far more consistently. Andy Greenberg's reporting in Wired profiled the company and the franchisees who installed the device, describing it as a tool that translated Taylor's diagnostic output into something a non-technical operator could act on directly.

Then, in early 2021, McDonald's issued a communication to franchisees warning them not to use the Kytch device, citing safety concerns. For O'Sullivan and Nelson, that letter was the clearest possible confirmation of what they had built into and why it mattered.

Kytch filed a lawsuit against Taylor Commercial Foodservice in Alameda County Superior Court in California. The complaint included a specific allegation: that Taylor had obtained a Kytch device through a McDonald's franchisee using an assumed identity, then studied the hardware. According to the complaint, Kytch's own servers logged activity from that device, and the data trail led directly back to Taylor. Taylor denied the allegation. [VERIFY: confirm this allegation appears in the filed complaint and note case number]

Taylor's counter-position was grounded in trade secret law. By tapping the machine's serial data port, Taylor argued, Kytch had accessed proprietary diagnostic information that belonged to Taylor. The case posed a question that existing law did not resolve cleanly: if a machine is installed in your restaurant under a contract you paid for, do you have a legal right to read data from its diagnostic interface? The answer depended entirely on how courts defined the boundary between a customer's operating rights and a manufacturer's intellectual property in firmware and diagnostic protocols.

By May 2021, the Federal Trade Commission had completed a report to Congress titled "Nixing the Fix," examining repair restrictions across American industries, from agricultural equipment to consumer electronics to food service machinery. The report concluded that found "scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions" and that restrictions were often implemented without demonstrable safety justification. [VERIFY: confirm exact FTC quote against "Nixing the Fix" PDF, pp. 1-10, and note specific page] Food service equipment appeared alongside John Deere tractors and medical devices, which was unusual. The policy conversation had rarely placed a soft-serve machine in the same frame as a combine harvester.

That report had no immediate enforcement mechanism. It was a study submitted to Congress, not a regulation. No statute changed in 2021 because of it. But it established that the federal government had reviewed the structure of what Taylor and McDonald's had built and found it worth putting in a congressional record.

The franchise-versus-corporate dimension of this story is what the right-to-repair framing tends to lose. McDonald's corporate earns royalties as a percentage of franchisee gross sales. It does not pay franchisee repair invoices. The approved-vendor system that produced the Taylor lock-in was designed to protect quality consistency: a McFlurry in Phoenix must taste like a McFlurry in Boston, and maintaining that across tens of thousands of independently owned locations requires locked-down supply chains. That objective is real and legitimate. The problem is that the mechanism built to protect quality also created a situation in which the entity absorbing the cost of broken machines had no contractual leverage to change who fixed them.

McDonald's subsequently began testing alternative soft-serve equipment suppliers at select U.S. locations. The company also worked with Taylor on firmware updates aimed at reducing the duration and frequency of the heat-treatment lockout. Neither change addressed the franchise governance architecture that made the problem structurally possible.

What the Kytch case produced was a documented evidentiary record. Before October 2020, the broken ice cream machine was a recurring joke: the subject of memes, a staple of late-night monologues, and a universal fast-food complaint with no paper trail. By 2022, there was a federal report, a civil lawsuit, detailed trade coverage, and a specific documented chain connecting the franchise agreement's vendor clause, to Taylor's service model, to the machine's diagnostic lockout. That is a different kind of change than a court ruling. A legal outcome can be appealed, reversed, or confined to one jurisdiction. A documented evidentiary record gets cited.

The franchise model that makes McDonald's one of the world's most profitable businesses is built on a principal-agent structure: corporate sets standards, franchisees execute them. When the vendor corporate approved to enforce those standards starts failing them, the franchisees carry the cost. The ice cream machine made that gap visible at a scale that could be measured and mapped in real time, by a website built in an afternoon. Kytch put a number on the cost. And whatever the final resolution of the Kytch litigation, the structural question it raised about who decides when an approved vendor is failing its customers will not leave the franchise disclosure conversation.
