On April 27, 2018, DocuSign opened on NASDAQ at $29 a share. Three and a half years later, in September 2021, the stock touched $310. The pandemic had remade DocuSign from a steady B2B e-signature company into one of the most valuable software stocks of the COVID era. Then the demand curve normalized. Eighteen months later, DocuSign traded at $39. The company had cycled through two CEOs, three rounds of layoffs, an activist investor, and a collapsed take-private bid. This video is about the math underneath that arc. It is not a video about e-signature. It is a video about the gap between subscription growth and subscription health.

DocuSign filed its first 10-K as a public company in March 2019. Annual revenue was $701 million, up 35% year over year. That was the steady SaaS profile. Two fiscal years later, the company reported $1.453 billion in revenue, up 49%. By fiscal 2022, the year that ended January 2022, revenue had reached $2.107 billion, up another 45%. Headcount tracked the curve. From roughly 3,600 employees at the IPO, DocuSign grew past 7,300 by early 2023. Most of that hiring landed in sales, customer success, and engineering. Each new salesperson was assumed to ramp within four to six quarters. Each engineer was building against a product roadmap drawn at peak demand. Then-CEO Dan Springer told investors on multiple earnings calls that the demand environment had structurally shifted.

175 million users by fiscal 2021. That was the metric Springer named on the Q4 fiscal 2021 earnings call in March 2021. DocuSign had added more users in eighteen months than it had in its first fifteen years combined. Enterprise customers, the higher-margin tier, grew 51% year over year. Contract durations stretched. Average contract value rose. Every leading indicator on the dashboard was pointing up. The Agreement Cloud rebrand, announced at the 2019 investor day, had positioned the company to capture an addressable market beyond e-signature. The pandemic delivered the demand without requiring the strategic execution.

The early signal was billings, not revenue. Billings are the leading indicator in a subscription business. They reflect contracts signed in the current period, not revenue ratably recognized from prior periods. By the second quarter of fiscal 2023, reported in September 2022, DocuSign billings growth had dropped into the single digits while revenue growth was still in the low twenties. The gap was the lag. Customers who had signed expensive long-term contracts in 2020 were renewing at lower seat counts or not renewing at all. The unit economics modeled at peak demand were drawn from cohorts that had been forced into the product by remote-work mandate, not by deliberate choice.

Inside DocuSign, the change was felt in the quarterly planning cycle. Revenue targets that had been raised through 2021 began holding flat. Hiring requisitions slowed and then froze. The product roadmap shifted from growth features to retention features. Sales compensation plans were rewritten to weight renewal performance over new logo acquisition. By the time the public financial statements caught up, the strategic posture had already changed inside the building.

In June 2022, Dan Springer stepped down. The official language was a mutual decision with the board. The substantive change came four months later. In October 2022, DocuSign announced Allan Thygesen as the new chief executive. Thygesen had spent fifteen years at Google, most recently running the Americas advertising business. DocuSign was reportedly his first public-company CEO role. The hiring of an ads operator to run a sales-led B2B SaaS company was itself a signal. The board was no longer optimizing for hyper-growth. It was optimizing for go-to-market efficiency.

Thygesen's first full earnings call was in March 2023, reporting fourth quarter fiscal 2023. The same call announced a layoff of roughly 10% of the workforce. The company framed the reduction as an operating-efficiency measure. The 10-K filed alongside the call told a more specific story. DocuSign had grown total operating expenses by more than 40% between fiscal 2021 and fiscal 2022, hiring against a demand curve that was already, in retrospect, peaking. The remediation began too late.

The math behind the layoffs is the structural lesson of this story. In subscription businesses, customer acquisition cost is paid up front. A customer brought in during a demand spike costs roughly the same to acquire as a customer brought in during a normal period. The difference shows up in retention. Customers who arrived because remote-work policy forced them into a product retain at lower rates than customers who chose the product on its merits. The CAC payback math assumes a baseline retention curve. When that curve breaks for a specific cohort, the cost is already booked. The revenue never arrives.

DocuSign was the largest publicly visible case of this dynamic in B2B SaaS. It was not the only one. The same pattern broke Peloton's subscription math. The Disclosed analysis of Peloton's churn, published earlier this week, describes the same fundamental dynamic in a consumer context. Different vertical, same arithmetic.

The 2024 sequence compounded the picture. In February 2024, DocuSign announced a second layoff round, reported at roughly 6% of staff. The phrasing in the filing was identical in structure to the 2023 round: a citation of operating discipline, a one-time charge for severance, a forward-looking commitment to efficiency. In spring 2024, the activist investor Elliott Management disclosed a position reported in the range of $1 billion and called for a strategic review. Elliott's playbook in software is well documented. Take a large position. Push for a sale process. Capture the premium. If the sale fails, push for board seats and operational changes that lift margin in the near term.

By March 2024, Reuters and the Wall Street Journal reported that Bain Capital and Hellman and Friedman were exploring a take-private transaction at a valuation near $16 billion. The talks collapsed over price. That valuation was a discount of more than 70% to DocuSign's 2021 peak market capitalization. The bidders walked anyway. The signal in that walk was that sophisticated capital, with full diligence access, could not underwrite the unit economics at that price. The public-market valuation continued to drift.

The 2025 state is a company that survived. DocuSign remains profitable on a non-GAAP basis. The cash position is healthy. The product is entrenched in enterprise workflows that do not unwind. Banks, real estate brokerages, and law firms have built procurement processes around it. The equity story has changed. Investors no longer model DocuSign as a hyper-growth platform. They model it as a mature B2B business with single-digit billings growth and a subscription base that requires active management of gross retention. The compensation philosophy has shifted from stock-based incentives tied to growth multiples to cash-and-equity packages weighted toward operational metrics.

The structural lesson is the lesson the pandemic delivered to every subscription business that grew through it. Net retention above 100% can mask gross retention deterioration. A platform with 110% net retention can be losing customers at 15% gross while expanding inside existing accounts. That math compounds over thirty-six months. By the time the layoff round is announced, the cohort damage is already booked. The financial calendar is the slowest measurement system in the company.
