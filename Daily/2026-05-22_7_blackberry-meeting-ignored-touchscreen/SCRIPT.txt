In January 2007, Mike Lazaridis was watching a video on his computer in Waterloo, Ontario. The video was the keynote that Steve Jobs had just delivered at Macworld in San Francisco, where Jobs introduced something called the iPhone. Lazaridis was the co-CEO of Research In Motion, the Canadian company that made the BlackBerry. RIM had eight million subscribers worldwide, dominated mobile email, and was, that month, the most valuable technology company in Canada by a wide margin. When Lazaridis finished watching the video, he found his co-CEO, Jim Balsillie, and showed him what Jobs had just announced. According to the 2015 book "Losing the Signal" by Jacquie McNish and Sean Silcoff, Balsillie listened, thought about it, and said, "It's OK. We'll be fine."

Six years later, BlackBerry's share of the global smartphone market was under one percent. The company that day was worth approximately seventy-five billion dollars at its peak. By 2014, it would post a single-year loss of five point nine billion dollars and shed nearly all of that valuation. This is the story of the meeting in Waterloo, and the years that followed.

In early 2007, the decision Balsillie made was not crazy. RIM was the dominant force in mobile communication. The BlackBerry Pearl had launched the previous September and was the company's first device aimed at consumers, not just enterprise users. The BlackBerry Curve was about to ship. RIM's keyboard was the reason a majority of all mobile email in North America passed through Waterloo. The phone Jobs had just announced did not have a keyboard. It had a piece of glass.

Lazaridis later told the New York Times why he thought the iPhone could not replace the BlackBerry. His words, in the article: "I couldn't type on it and I still can't type on it, and a lot of my friends can't type on it. It's hard to type on a piece of glass." The keyboard was not just a feature. It was the identity of the product, and the identity of the company. RIM had spent more than a decade optimizing the physical relationship between a thumb and a key. Apple had thrown that out and asked customers to learn to type on glass.

But Lazaridis did not entirely believe his own argument. According to "Losing the Signal," when he eventually peered inside an actual iPhone unit later in 2007, he turned to his engineers and said, "They've put a Mac in this thing." He understood, privately, that the iPhone was not really a phone. It was a small computer that happened to make calls. RIM's product was a phone that happened to send email. The category had shifted under them. He told his engineers, in another moment recorded in the book, "We have to fix everything that's wrong with the iPhone."

The public company line stayed dismissive. BlackBerry chief operating officer Larry Conlee characterized the iPhone in the same book as a device with "rapid battery drain and a lousy keyboard." The reasoning was reasonable for the position RIM held in early 2007. The cost of a wrong call in either direction looked symmetrical. Pivot too fast, abandon the keyboard advantage, and you give competitors permission to flank you on the thing your customers actually want. Pivot too slow, and the new category eats you.

By 2008, the iPhone had captured roughly a fifth of the United States smartphone market. RIM's market share was still rising in absolute terms because the smartphone market itself was expanding rapidly. The data could be read either way. Either the iPhone was a niche product for tech enthusiasts, or it was the early signal of a category replacement. Inside RIM, both readings had defenders.

On June 19, 2008, RIM's stock closed at one hundred forty-seven dollars and fifty-five cents. The company's market capitalization that day reached approximately seventy-five billion dollars. It was the highest the company would ever be valued. Eighteen months had passed since Balsillie said they would be fine.

In November 2008, RIM launched its response. The device was called the BlackBerry Storm. It was a touchscreen phone, the company's first, designed to compete head-to-head with the iPhone. Verizon Wireless launched it aggressively in the United States. Storm units sold quickly out of the gate.

Then they came back. The screen on Storm was a piece of glass mounted on a mechanical clicker so users would still feel a physical button press. The design was a compromise between the keyboard heritage and the touchscreen future. It worked badly. Reviews were brutal. The software was unfinished. Verizon reportedly returned hundreds of thousands of units. The product that was supposed to fix everything wrong with the iPhone shipped with everything wrong with itself.

By 2009, BlackBerry still held about fifty-five percent of the United States smartphone market. The Storm had not killed the company. It had bought RIM another year of dominance in the enterprise segment, where the keyboard still mattered, the encryption still mattered, and the IT department was still in charge of phone choices. Inside that pocket of the market, the BlackBerry was untouchable.

Outside it, the iPhone was eating everything. Apple released the iPhone 3GS in June 2009. Google's Android, launched in late 2008, began consolidating the rest of the consumer market. By 2010, RIM's global share had peaked at roughly twenty percent and was beginning to decline. By 2011 the slide had accelerated. By 2013 BlackBerry's global smartphone market share had fallen to about zero point six percent.

In January 2013, after years of delay, RIM launched a new operating system and a new flagship phone called the Z10. It was a full touchscreen device with a modern app environment. The company also launched a parallel device called the Q10, which kept the physical keyboard. The Q10 was the bet that there was still an audience that wanted the BlackBerry as it had been. There was not. By the end of fiscal 2014, the company had posted a five point nine billion dollar loss, written off massive unsold inventory, and replaced its CEO.

The lesson is not that Balsillie and Lazaridis were stupid in January 2007. They were running the most successful Canadian technology company in history. They had a real edge in keyboard engineering, enterprise security, and battery life. They had a customer base that genuinely valued those things. The lesson is what happens when the customer you optimized for ten years ago is no longer the customer the market is being built around.

We covered Patagonia last week. Yvon Chouinard kept making decisions that looked like they would cost his company short-term revenue. They did not. RIM made decisions that looked like they were protecting the business they had. They were protecting the wrong business. Patagonia bet against its own product and won. BlackBerry bet on the comfort of the existing product and lost. The shape of the decision was the same. The direction was opposite. There are more case studies like this one already in the queue.
