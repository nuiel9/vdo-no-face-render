## Description

In 1956, a designer named Gillis Lundgren could not fit a table into his car. He removed the legs. What happened next built one of the most studied retail models in history. This case study is not about the parking lot moment. It is about the 13 years of constraint that made Ingvar Kamprad ready to see it, and the behavioral economics principle, documented by Harvard Business School researchers in 2012, that explains why the model still works today. Sources: IKEA corporate history portal, Kamprad's 1976 Testament of a Furniture Dealer, Norton, Mochon and Ariely's IKEA Effect study.

## Pinned Comment

The behavioral economics paper referenced in this video is Norton, Mochon, and Ariely (2012), "The IKEA Effect: When Labor Leads to Love," published in the Journal of Consumer Psychology. The core finding: people who assemble an object themselves rate it as equal in quality to objects made by experts. Kamprad's 1976 "Testament of a Furniture Dealer" predated this research by 36 years. Whether he intuited it or simply discovered it through trial and error, the result was the same: a model that transfers cost, increases attachment, and reduces returns simultaneously. The Billy bookcase (1979, Gillis Lundgren) is the clearest single proof point.
