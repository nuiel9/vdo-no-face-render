In December 1950, a French industrialist named Marcel Bich opened a factory in Clichy, just north of Paris, and started selling a ballpoint pen for less than one franc. The patent was not his. The technology was not new. Three American companies had already tried to commercialize the same kind of pen and failed. Bich did something different. He made the pen cheap enough to throw away. Seventy-six years later, his company posted 2.19 billion euros in revenue. The Cristal pen he launched that month has now sold well over 100 billion units worldwide.

The patent belonged to László Bíró, a Hungarian newspaper editor who had worked in Budapest in the 1930s. Bíró noticed that newspaper printing ink dried within seconds, while fountain pen ink stayed wet on the page for almost a minute. With his brother György, a chemist, he combined a printing ink formulation with a small rotating ball at the pen tip. The Argentine patent was filed in 1943, after the brothers had fled Hungary via Paris and Madrid. Bíró sold the US rights to Eberhard Faber for about half a million dollars. Eberhard Faber sold them on to Eversharp for two million. Both companies marketed the pen as a luxury writing instrument and priced it at twelve to fifteen dollars. In 1946 that was about two weeks of factory wages. The pens leaked. The ink dried in storage.

By 1950 the American ballpoint market had collapsed. Reynolds International Pen Company, which had launched its first US ballpoint at Gimbel's department store in New York in October 1945, was effectively out of the business within four years. Parker Pen Company stayed out of the ballpoint market entirely for most of the 1950s after watching the Reynolds collapse.

Bich licensed the European rights from Bíró around 1949. He did not try to fix the marketing. He attacked the unit cost.

In Clichy, Bich applied tools borrowed from Swiss watchmaking. He manufactured a tungsten carbide ball with a tolerance of one one-hundredth of a millimeter. That ball was the engineering bottleneck. Without it, ink delivery was uneven and the pen skipped. Bich also developed an injection molding process, run by a sister company called Décolletage Plastique, that produced the hex-shaped polystyrene barrel and the brass tip in a single coordinated run. By the second year of production, the Clichy factory was assembling roughly 240,000 pens per day.

The design choices were as deliberate as the manufacturing math. The hex barrel matched the diameter of a standard wooden pencil so existing pencil holders worked. The transparent polystyrene let the user see exactly how much ink was left. The plastic cap had a small hole drilled through it as a safety feature in case a child swallowed it. None of these were luxury features. They were chosen because they cost almost nothing to add and they made the pen feel respectable despite the price.

The disposability bet was confirmed within five years. By 1955 Bic was selling roughly 350 million pens annually. The cost per unit had fallen below twenty centimes. The luxury pen incumbents, Mont Blanc and Waterman, kept selling at premium prices but stopped competing on the same shelf as Bic. They moved up-market and ceded the volume tier entirely. By 1953 the Cristal had displaced the Eversharp ballpoint in France and pushed into Italy and the United Kingdom. The cliffhanger here is not whether the pen succeeded. It is what Bich did next.

The cost-collapse logic was not really about pens. It was about any product where consumers had been trained to pay for permanence.

In 1958, Bich entered the United States by acquiring the Waterman Pen Company and forming the Waterman-Bic Pen Corporation, with its US headquarters in Milford, Connecticut. The acquisition gave Bic an existing distribution network and avoided import friction. By 1967 the Cristal was the best-selling pen in the United States.

In 1973, twenty-three years after the Cristal launched, Bic introduced a disposable lighter. The market at the time was dominated by Zippo, Ronson, and Dunhill, all positioned as lifetime objects, often refillable, sometimes engraved. The Bic lighter cost roughly the price of a pack of cigarettes. By 1979 the company had sold its one billionth lighter, according to its own corporate history. In the 2024 fiscal year, the Flame for Life division generated 810 million euros in net sales, making it the largest of the three segments.

In 1975, Bic entered the shaving category with a single-blade disposable razor. Gillette had spent decades positioning the safety razor as a precision instrument, and locking customers into a permanent revenue stream by selling cheap handles and expensive blade refills. Bic broke the model by selling the whole razor as a throwaway. Gillette responded with its own disposable Good News razor in 1976, but ceded the bottom of the market to Bic. In 2024, the Blade Excellence division produced 543 million euros in net sales. Stationery, the original business, contributed about 700 million euros that same year. The pen that started everything is now the third leg of the portfolio, not the largest.

In 1992, Bic acquired Wite-Out, the US correction fluid brand, extending the stationery line beyond writing into the same cheap-disposable-volume logic. Société M.B.D., the Bich family holding, retains majority voting control of the public company on Euronext Paris through a dual-class structure that separates voting rights from economic interest. The trash-pen company has been a family-controlled public company for the entire seventy-six year ride.

The full 2024 picture is in the company's Q4 results press release, issued through Euronext Paris on February 18th, 2025. The company's own characterization, quoted from the release: "strong 2024 adjusted EBIT of 343 million euros, growing 3.0% year-on-year." And later: "outstanding free cash flow generation at 271 million euros during 2024, up 9% year-on-year." Total revenue was 2.19 billion euros, down from 2.26 billion in 2023. The cash result was not driven by revenue growth. It was driven by margin expansion. That is the same lever Bich pulled in 1950.

By 2004, the Museum of Modern Art in New York acquired the Bic Cristal for its permanent collection. The acquisition was part of a curatorial framing called humble masterpieces, alongside the Post-it Note and the IKEA Allen wrench. In September 2006, Bic announced that the 100 billionth Cristal had been sold.

We covered McDonald's broken ice cream machines two weeks ago. That was a margin-thin business that never controlled the mechanics of its own equipment. Bic is the opposite outcome. The mechanics of the pen, the lighter, and the razor were the entire moat, engineered cheap enough that disposability became an asset rather than a defect.
