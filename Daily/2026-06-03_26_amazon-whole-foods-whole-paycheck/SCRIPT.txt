# SCRIPT: #26 Amazon / Whole Foods 'Whole Paycheck'

Working title: Why Couldn't Amazon Make Whole Foods Cheap?

Total narration words: 1114 (Part 1: 487 / Part 2: 627). Target ~8:15 at ~141 wpm Algieba.

## PART 1: THE PROMISE


[Scene 1 | high] Hook: Aug 28 2017, $13.7B, day-one cuts
Lower-third: Aug 28 2017 · $13.7B / Salmon $15 to $10/lb
On August 28th, 2017, Amazon officially took ownership of Whole Foods Market. It had paid thirteen point seven billion dollars. And on that very first morning, it did something the grocery industry had been waiting years to see. It cut prices. Fresh Atlantic salmon dropped from fifteen dollars a pound to ten. Organic avocados fell by almost half. On some items the cuts ran as deep as forty-three percent. The message was simple. The era of Whole Paycheck was over.

[Scene 2 | medium] Thesis: the price-cutter met its limit
Lower-third: $13.7 billion deal / 'Whole Paycheck' still here
That was the promise. Amazon, the most ruthless price-cutter in the history of retail, had bought the most expensive grocery chain in America, and it was going to fix the one thing everyone complained about. Seven years later, the nickname is still there, and your bill at Whole Foods is higher than it was the day Amazon walked in. This is the story of why the company that destroyed pricing in every other category could not destroy it here.

[Scene 3 | medium] What 'Whole Paycheck' meant: Austin 1980
Lower-third: Whole Foods · Austin · 1980 / 'Whole Paycheck'
To understand the promise, you have to understand the nickname. Whole Foods was founded in Austin, Texas in 1980 by a man named John Mackey. Over the next three decades it became the temple of organic groceries. Polished produce, hand-lettered chalkboard signs, grass-fed everything. It also became famous for prices so high that shoppers gave it a nickname. Whole Paycheck. The joke was that one bag of groceries could cost you exactly that.

[Scene 4 | low] Why Amazon wanted it: June 16 2017
Lower-third: June 16 2017 · $42/share / 450+ stores acquired
On June 16th, 2017, Amazon announced it would buy the chain for forty-two dollars a share. For Amazon it was a way into a business it had failed to crack for a decade. Physical grocery. More than four hundred and fifty stores, most of them in wealthy neighborhoods, handed over almost overnight. Wall Street understood the threat immediately. The day the deal was announced, shares of every major grocery competitor fell.

[Scene 5 | medium] The day-one cuts were real
Lower-third: Up to 43% off · day one / Bananas · eggs · salmon
Then came the price cuts, and they were real. Bloomberg documented reductions of as much as forty-three percent on the first day. Bananas, eggs, salmon, avocados. Amazon put up bright signs in the stores. It was doing exactly what it had done to books, to electronics, to everything it touched. Drive the price down, take the customer, worry about profit later.

[Scene 6 | medium] Prime integration: May 2018
Lower-third: May 2018 · Prime / Extra 10% off
In May of 2018, Amazon went further. Prime members would get an extra ten percent off sale items at Whole Foods, marked with yellow signs throughout the store. Sign in with your Amazon account, scan a code at checkout, save more. The two companies were fusing. The richest data engine in the world now knew exactly what was in your grocery cart.

[Scene 7 | high] Cliffhanger: the receipts disagreed
Lower-third: Analysts priced the baskets / The receipts disagreed
So the logic seemed airtight. The cheapest operator in retail now owned the most expensive grocer in America. Whole Paycheck was a dead brand walking. There was only one problem with that story. When analysts actually went into the stores, week after week, and added up the receipts, the numbers told them something nobody expected.

## PART 2: THE REVERSAL


[Scene 8 | high] First crack: Gordon Haskett, 108 items, -40c
Lower-third: 108 items · minus $0.40 / $408.23 to $407.83
A research firm called Gordon Haskett did something simple. It sent people into a Whole Foods in Princeton, New Jersey, and priced the same basket of goods over and over. About a year after the Amazon deal, it found that a basket of a hundred and eight items had fallen by all of forty cents. Not forty cents an item. Forty cents total. From four hundred and eight dollars and twenty-three cents, to four hundred and seven dollars and eighty-three cents. The firm's analyst, Chuck Grom, put it plainly. Many of the aggressive price actions from Whole Foods, he wrote, have been more bark than bite.

[Scene 9 | medium] Still whole paycheck: $195, 13% above
Lower-third: 60-item basket · $195 / Protein 28% pricier
The cuts that did happen were narrow. They hit the commodities you could compare anywhere. Bananas, eggs, the things with a known price. But by the summer of 2018, a basket of sixty everyday items at Whole Foods still cost a hundred and ninety-five dollars. About thirteen percent more than a conventional supermarket. Protein was twenty-eight percent more expensive. The store still cost a whole paycheck, because Amazon had only cut the prices that were easy to cut.

[Scene 10 | medium] The reversal: early 2019, prices rise
Lower-third: Early 2019 · prices rise / ~700 contracts expired
Then the prices started going back up. In early 2019, Whole Foods confirmed it was raising them. Roughly seven hundred supplier contracts that had locked in lower prices were expiring. Hundreds of items went up, by an average of sixty-six cents each. Crackers, soap, cookies, ice cream. The company blamed its suppliers, saying it had absorbed inflation in materials, labor, and transportation. The most powerful retailer on earth was now explaining to customers why their groceries cost more.

[Scene 11 | medium] The structural why: cost baked in
Lower-third: Premium sourcing · prime rent / Cost baked into product
Here is the part most coverage missed. Amazon never actually failed. It did exactly what it knows how to do. It cut the price of commodities to the bone. But Whole Foods was never expensive because of its bananas. It was expensive because of what it was. Small-batch organic suppliers, strict quality standards, prime real estate, higher labor costs, a supply chain built for premium, not for volume. You cannot data-optimize your way out of that. The cost was baked into the product.

[Scene 12 | medium] Reframe: expensive WAS the product
Lower-third: Expensive WAS the product
Which means Whole Paycheck was never a mistake to be fixed. It was the business. People who shopped at Whole Foods were, on some level, paying to shop at Whole Foods. Amazon bought a brand whose entire identity was being expensive, and then promised to make it cheap. Those two things could not both survive.

[Scene 13 | low] Founder out: Mackey retires, Sept 2022
Lower-third: Sept 2022 · Mackey retires / 42 years
In September of 2022, John Mackey retired. Forty-two years after he opened the first store. The founder was gone. Amazon had full control. And the chain he built still sat where it always had. At the top of the price chart.

[Scene 14 | medium] Where it stands: ~1% vs Walmart ~20%
Lower-third: Whole Foods about 1% / Walmart about 20%
Today, Whole Foods holds about one percent of the American grocery market. Walmart holds roughly twenty. The price war that Amazon supposedly entered in 2017, it is winning almost nowhere, and certainly not through Whole Foods. What Amazon actually got for its thirteen point seven billion dollars was the real estate, the Prime members, and the data. The low prices it promised at the register never really arrived.

[Scene 15 | high] Close: Costco mirror + caveat + CTA
Lower-third: Costco: cheap is the product / Subscribe · Disclosed
We covered Costco a few days ago. A company whose entire model is built on selling things at almost no margin on purpose. Costco is cheap because being cheap is the product. Whole Foods is the mirror image. It is expensive because being expensive is the product. Amazon, for all its power, could not change which kind of company it had bought. The lesson is older than e-commerce. Sometimes a company's greatest weakness is just its actual product, wearing a different coat. There are more case studies like this one already in the queue.
