===== PART 1 =====

[Scene 1 | high] Hook: Nov 15 2022 Eras Tour presale meltdown
OVERLAYS: Nov 15 2022 | 3.5 million fans, one website
On the morning of November fifteenth, 2022, three and a half million people sat refreshing the same website. They were all trying to buy tickets to see one artist. Within an hour, the site buckled. Fans were frozen in endless queues, logged out, locked in digital waiting rooms that never moved. By the end of the day the company had sold two point four million tickets, a record, and enraged almost everyone who tried. But this was not a glitch. This was the machine working exactly as designed.

[Scene 2 | medium] Thesis: ruled a monopoly, fees still won't drop
OVERLAYS: April 15 2026: ruled a monopoly | The fees still won't drop
Three and a half years later, in April of 2026, a federal jury reached a verdict. The ticketing company and its parent had operated an illegal monopoly. The company lost on every count. And here is the strange part. Even now, your fees are not going down. This is the story of how the most hated checkout screen in America was built, and why winning in a courtroom cannot take it apart.

[Scene 3 | medium] The fee everyone knows: GAO avg 27%, up to 58%
OVERLAYS: Service + facility + processing fees | GAO 2018: avg 27%, up to 58%
You already know the feeling. The ticket says ninety dollars. Then comes the service fee, the facility fee, the order processing fee, and somehow you are paying a hundred and thirty. In 2018, the Government Accountability Office studied this. On primary ticket sales, fees averaged twenty-seven percent of the ticket price. On some events they ran as high as fifty-eight percent. The real question is why no competitor ever undercut it.

[Scene 4 | medium] How the machine was built: Jan 25 2010 merger
OVERLAYS: Jan 25 2010 | Ticketing + concerts merge
The answer starts on January twenty-fifth, 2010. That day, the Justice Department cleared a merger between the two giants of live music. One side controlled the ticketing. The other controlled the concerts, the tours, and a vast network of venues. Regulators were nervous enough that they forced the combined company to sign a ten-year consent decree, a written promise not to abuse its power. The promise, it turned out, was the easy part.

[Scene 5 | medium] The flywheel: promotion -> venues -> ticketing
OVERLAYS: Promotion -> venues -> ticketing | No rival checkout
What the merger created was a flywheel. The company promotes the tour. It books that tour into venues it owns or controls. Those venues are ticketed by its own platform. And every ticket carries the fees. An artist who wants the biggest stages, and a fan who wants the biggest shows, both end up inside the same machine. There is no rival checkout to click over to. When you control every step, the fee is not competition. It is a toll.

[Scene 6 | medium] Consent decree violations: 5 found, 2020 extended $3M
OVERLAYS: 5 violations found | 2020: decree extended, $3M
The consent decree was supposed to stop exactly this. It did not. By 2019, the Justice Department had identified five separate violations, conduct it said was repeated over years, including pressure on venues that dared to use a different ticketer. The penalty, in 2020, was to extend the decree to 2025 and charge the company three million dollars. For a business this size, it was a parking ticket. The machine kept turning.

[Scene 7 | high] Eras Tour mechanics: Verified Fan, site crashed
OVERLAYS: Verified Fan presale | Site crashed within the hour
Which brings us back to that morning in November 2022. The company had invited three and a half million people into a presale it called Verified Fan, a system meant to keep the bots out and let real fans in. Instead, the site crashed within the hour. But the fans who got through were not paying the price on the ticket. They were paying the price, plus the toll.

[Scene 8 | medium] General sale canceled: the mask slips
OVERLAYS: General sale canceled | 'Insufficient inventory'
Two days later, the company did something almost unheard of. It canceled the general public sale entirely, blaming extraordinarily high demand and not enough tickets left. Millions of people who had done nothing wrong were simply shut out. For the first time, the country was not just annoyed at a checkout screen. It was asking a much bigger question. How did one company end up holding the only door into live music?

===== PART 2 =====

[Scene 9 | medium] Senate Judiciary hearing Jan 24 2023
OVERLAYS: Jan 24 2023 | Senate Judiciary Committee
On January twenty-fourth, 2023, a company executive sat down in front of the Senate Judiciary Committee. It was one of the rare days in Washington when both parties agreed on something. Senators from the left and the right took turns describing the same experience. Their constituents, their staff, themselves, all crushed by the same fees and the same frozen screens. The company blamed bots and scalpers. The senators were not convinced.

[Scene 10 | medium] DOJ + 40 states sue: May 23 2024
OVERLAYS: May 23 2024 | DOJ + ~40 states sue
On May twenty-third, 2024, the Justice Department, joined by roughly forty states and the District of Columbia, filed suit. The goal was blunt. They wanted to break the company apart, to pull the ticketing arm back out of the concert empire. Their argument was the flywheel itself. A single firm controlling promotion, venues, and ticketing, they said, was not a competitor that had won. It was a monopoly that had locked the doors.

[Scene 11 | medium] The case: monopolization + illegal bundling
OVERLAYS: Monopolized primary ticketing | Illegal bundling
The case described a system where the parts protected each other. Use our ticketing, or you might not get our tours. Play our venues, or lose access to the biggest acts. The Justice Department called it illegal bundling, tying one service to another so that no rival could ever compete on just a single piece. The toll was not a price. It was a trap.

[Scene 12 | medium] March 2026: DOJ settles, no breakup
OVERLAYS: March 2026: DOJ settles | ~$200M, no breakup
Then, in March of 2026, the federal case took a turn. The Justice Department settled. The company agreed to about two hundred million dollars for the states and a set of structural changes, but it would not be broken up. The single most important demand, splitting the ticketing arm back off, was gone. For the company, it looked like survival. But not every plaintiff signed. Several states refused the deal and pressed on alone.

[Scene 13 | high] April 15 2026: jury liable on every count
OVERLAYS: April 15 2026 | Liable on every count
Those states went to trial. And on April fifteenth, 2026, the jury came back. The two companies were liable on every antitrust count, found to have illegally monopolized ticketing and overcharged the public. It was the verdict fans had wanted for a decade. The company that built the toll had finally been told, in a court of law, that the toll was illegal. And still, the fees did not move.

[Scene 14 | medium] Structural truth: the fee is the business model
OVERLAYS: The fee is the business model | Not a bug to fix
Here is why. A guilty verdict can punish a company. It cannot, by itself, un-build a structure. As long as the same firm promotes the show, owns the venue, and sells the ticket, the fee is simply how that structure makes money. It is not bolted onto the business. It is the business. To actually lower the fee, someone has to take the machine apart, and that fight, over the remedy, is still being decided by a judge.

[Scene 15 | medium] Cross-ref: the thing you hate is the model (Whole Foods)
OVERLAYS: The thing you hate is the model | See: Whole Foods on Disclosed
We have seen this exact shape before on this channel. When Amazon bought Whole Foods, everyone expected the high prices to fall, and they did not, because the premium was never a flaw. It was the product. Ticketmaster is the same lesson in a different costume. The fee you hate is not the company failing to serve you. It is the company doing exactly what its structure was built to do.
